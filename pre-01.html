Pre 1 · What “Jianghu” Means

Here is a number to start with. At the close of September 28, 2026, the Shanghai and Shenzhen exchanges together listed 5,222 A-share companies — 2,320 in Shanghai, 2,902 in Shenzhen — with a combined market value of ¥124.8 trillion.

Sit with that for a second. This is one of the largest equity markets on earth by any measure you like — companies, listings, money at stake — and if you read financial English for a living, chances are you have almost never read a sentence about what actually happened in it on a particular Tuesday. Big names arrive filtered through a handful of ETF tickers. Headlines arrive only when something breaks. The daily texture — who bought, who walked away, which corner of the board caught fire at two in the afternoon — never arrives at all.

This publication is an attempt to fix that, one day at a time. Every trading day, after the close, I write down what happened in this market and why, in English, for readers who do not follow it. That is the whole ambition. It is not a small one, but it is a plain one.

The title needs explaining, because half of it does not translate.

Jianghu (江湖) is two characters: river, lake. Taken literally it means something close to "the waterways" — and in old Chinese usage it came to mean the world outside officialdom, the loose country of wanderers, martial artists, merchants, fortune tellers and people who lived by their wits rather than by salary. It is the setting of every wuxia story ever told, and it survives in modern speech as the word for any field where people compete under rules nobody wrote down.

Which is, not accidentally, a fair description of a stock market. Half of what governs prices here is printed in exchange rulebooks; the other half is habit, reputation, crowd behaviour and memory of what happened the last time something like this occurred. A trader in Shanghai saying "the jianghu has changed" is not being poetic. He is saying the unwritten rules moved and he intends to move with them.

The other half of the title is older and belongs to English. The tape is what old traders called the stream of prices coming off the telegraph, and "reading the tape" still means reading what prices are doing rather than what people say about them. It is a slightly unfashionable word now, which is partly why I like it. There is nothing glamorous about a tape. It just runs, and you read it.

So: The Jianghu Tape. A daily read of the Chinese tape. The local word for the arena, bolted onto the old word for the record. I am aware the combination is odd. It is meant to be.

My name is Dan Rivers and I watch this market every day. I am not a broker, not a fund manager, and I do not hold a securities investment advisory licence anywhere. I have no product to sell you, take no payment to mention anything, and will not be paid to. When a company's name appears here it appears as a fact of the day's trading — it closed up, it sealed at its daily cap, it led its sector — and never as an idea about what you should do about it.

I want to be precise about why I am telling you this in the fourth paragraph rather than in the small print, because it is not politeness. Under United States law, a periodical that publishes general market commentary is not an investment adviser — and that exemption holds only if what it publishes really is general commentary. It has to be written for everyone rather than for your particular situation, it has to be free of the author's own stake in what he is describing, and it has to appear on a regular schedule rather than being timed to what some stock is about to do. Three conditions, easy to state and easy to break by accident. The promises in the next section exist to keep them intact.

A disclaimer at the bottom does not change what a piece of writing is. What it says in the middle does.

What you will get. On each trading day, one piece: what happened in the market that afternoon, why it happened as far as anyone can honestly tell, and how today resembles earlier days whose endings are already known. That last part is the one I care about most. This market has been through a great deal in thirty-odd years, and most afternoons have a precedent. Knowing the precedent does not tell you what comes next — nothing does — but it is a better companion than a hunch.

What you will not get. No forecasts about tomorrow, no price targets, no probabilities dressed up as analysis, and nothing that tells you to buy, sell, add, cut or hold anything. No "watch this name," no "the sector is set to run." Companies appear as facts; the future does not appear at all. If you write to me asking whether something suits you, I will not answer — the answer would be personal advice, and this is not a place that gives it.

What the numbers mean. Chinese markets quote gains in red and losses in green, the reverse of the convention in New York and London. Most English coverage deals with this by quietly recoding everything to Western colours, which I think is a mistake — it hides a thing worth knowing. I keep the local convention and mark direction with arrows instead: ↑ for up, ↓ for down. You will also meet terms like limit-up — the 10 per cent daily cap on most A-share moves — and turnover, by which I mean the combined Shanghai–Shenzhen trading value for the session. Each gets explained the first time it appears.

How the calendar works. A-shares are closed this week for the National Day holiday, so there are no sessions to report and nothing to pretend otherwise. Until October 8 you will get one short piece a day — seven in total — covering the machinery instead: the daily price caps, the settlement rule that makes tomorrow a different country, why red means up, who actually trades here, and one week in 2024 that this market still talks about. On October 8 the exchanges reopen and the daily chapters begin.

If any of that sounds useful, subscribe. It costs nothing and there is nothing behind the paywall, because there never will be. The plan is simply to be here, every trading day, with an honest account of what happened — written at the close, not rewritten afterwards to look clever.

The record shows where the road has been, not where it goes next.

开宗明义。先给一个数字。2026年9月28日收盘，沪深两市共有A股上市公司5,222家——沪市2,320家、深市2,902家——合计总市值124.8万亿元。

这个数字值得停一下。无论按公司家数、挂牌数量还是牵涉的资金算，这都是世界上最大的股票市场之一；可是如果你以读英文财经为业，你几乎从未读过一句关于它某个周二到底发生了什么的话。大公司的名字隔着几只ETF的行情传过来；只有在什么东西碎掉的时候，新闻标题才到来。而日常的质地——谁在买、谁走了、下午两点哪个角落烧起来了——根本没有抵达。

这份刊物想做的就是补上这件事，一天一次。每个交易日收盘后，我把这个市场当天发生的事与原因用英文写下来，写给并不跟踪它的读者。全部野心就这么多。不算小，但很朴素。

名字。这个标题需要解释，因为它有一半是翻不过来的。

江湖，两个字：江、湖。字面是水路；在古汉语里渐渐指官府之外那个松散的世界——遊侠、武人、商贩、算命的，以及一切凭本事吃饭而不是凭俸禄吃饭的人。它是所有武侠故事发生的那个场域，也活在今天的口语里，用来指任何一群人按没人写下来的规则互相竞争的场子。

这——并非巧合——也是对股票市场的公道描述。这里决定价格的东西，一半印在交易所的规则里，另一半是习惯、名声、从众，以及对上一次类似情形怎么收场的记忆。上海的交易员说"江湖变了"，他不是在抒情，他是在说那些没写下来的规则移动了，而他打算跟着动。

标题的另一半属于英文，而且更老。tape 是老派交易员对电报机上淌出来的那串价格的叫法；reading the tape 至今仍指"读价格正在做什么"，而不是读人们说价格在做什么。这个词如今有点不时髦，这正是我选它的部分原因。tape 没什么光鲜的，它只是一直流，你只是一直读。

所以：The Jianghu Tape。一份中文市场日志。当地的词（场子）配上老派的词（记录）。我知道这两者拼在一起有点怪。本来就该怪。

我是谁。我叫 Dan Rivers，每天盯这个市场。不是券商，不是基金经理，在任何地方都没有证券投资咨询资质。没有东西卖给你，不为提及任何标的收过钱，将来也不收。当一个公司的名字出现在这里，它是作为当天盘面的事实出现的——它收涨、它封住当日上限、它领涨所在板块——从来不是作为一个关于你该怎么办的主意。

我要说清楚为什么这些话写在这里而不是写在页脚小字里，因为这不是客气。按美国法律，刊登泛财经评论的定期出版物不属于投资顾问——而这个豁免成立的前提，是它刊登的东西真的是面向普遍的评论。它必须是写给所有人的，而不是写给你的具体处境的；必须不含作者本人在所描述标的上的利益；必须按固定节奏出现，而不是掐着某只股票即将发生的事来择时。三个条件，说来容易，一不小心也很容易破。下面那些承诺就是为了让它们不失守。

写什么，不写什么。你会得到的：每个交易日一篇——当天下午市场发生了什么、就人们能诚实判断的范围内为什么发生、以及今天像不像历史上那些结局已经知道的旧日子。最后这一点是我最看重的。这个市场三十多年里经历过很多事，多数下午都有先例。知道先例并不能告诉你接下来会发生什么——没有什么能——但它比直觉是个更好的旅伴。

你不会得到的：不预测明天，不给目标价，不给伪装成分析的概率，也没有任何告诉你买、卖、加、减、持有某物的句子。没有"关注这只票"，没有"这个板块要启动了"。公司名字作为事实出现，未来压根不出现。你写信问我某样东西适不适合你，我不会回答——回答就是个人化建议，而这里不是给个人建议的地方。

数字的读法：中国市场涨用红、跌用绿，与纽约伦敦相反。多数英文报道的处理方式是悄悄改回西方配色，我认为这是个错误——它把一个值得知道的东西藏起来了。我保留本地惯例，改用箭头标方向：↑ 涨、↓ 跌。你还会遇到 limit-up（多数A股每日10%涨幅上限）与 turnover（我用来指当日沪深两市合计成交额）这类词，首次出现都会解释。

日历怎么走：本周A股因国庆假期休市，没有交易日可记，也不必假装有。10月8日之前，每天一篇短文，共七篇，讲的是机器本身——每日涨跌幅上限、那条把明天变成另一个国家的结算规则、为什么红是涨、究竟谁在这儿交易，以及2024年那个至今还被这个市场提起的一周。10月8日交易所开门，每日章节开始。

如果这些听起来有用，就订阅。免费，付费墙后面什么也没有，因为永远不会有任何东西在后面。计划很简单：每个交易日都在这里，交出一份诚实的当日记录——收盘时写下的，事后不为了显得聪明而改写。
