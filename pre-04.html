ONE The Day the Screen Turned Green Opening
On 3 Feb 2020,
the index fell
7.72 percent.
The screen was green.

Monday, 3 February 2020, was the first session after an extended Spring Festival break, and the Shanghai Composite closed at 2,746.61 — down 7.72 percent on the day. Across the two exchanges, 3,016 stocks finished limit-down, sealed at the floor with sellers queued behind them. It was one of the worst sessions in the market's history.

And the screens that morning did not look like a bad day. They looked like spring.

Here, a rising price is printed in red and a falling one in green — the reverse of the palette most readers elsewhere grew up with. On a day when three thousand stocks were pinned at the bottom of their permitted range, the board was a field of green. Nothing on it was wrong; everything on it was upside down to foreign eyes. That inversion is the first thing a reader has to unlearn, and it is worth unlearning carefully, because the colour is not decoration. It is the first layer of a local grammar, and the rest of the screen is built on top of it.

TWO Two Palettes, One Argument Convention
In English,
a loss is
"in the red."
In Shanghai, red is the gain.

Mainland China, Hong Kong and Taiwan paint a rise red and a fall green. The United States and most of Europe do the opposite: green for the gain, red for the loss. The two systems meet constantly — in the same newsroom, in the same terminal, sometimes in the same sentence — and they do not agree.

There is no founding document for the split, and the explanations usually offered are cultural rather than technical. Red is the colour of celebration in Chinese tradition, the colour of a wedding and of a good opening day; a shop that loses money is, in English, in the red, while the dollar itself is green. Each palette is coherent inside its own language. They simply disagree at the border.

The consequence is practical, not philosophical. A reader who glances at a Hong Kong screen on an American habit will read a rally as a rout and a rout as a rally, and will be wrong in the same direction every time. This chronicle therefore prints an arrow beside every move — ↑ and ↓ — so that direction never depends on which palette a reader was raised in. The colour is local; the arrow is not.

THREE How to Read a Screen The grammar
White line, weighted.
Yellow line, not.
When they part,
the tape is arguing.

The intraday chart carries two lines. The white one is the weighted index, the number everyone quotes. The yellow one is the unweighted average of the same universe of stocks — what the session looks like if every name carries the same vote. When the yellow line rides above the white, the broad crowd is doing better than the index admits. When it sags below, a handful of heavyweights is holding the number up while the rest of the market walks downhill, and the headline figure is the least honest thing on the screen.

Along the bottom, the bars are turnover by minute, coloured by direction. Read them as breath: a rising price on thinning bars is a different animal from a rising price on thickening ones. The comparison is always against the same hour of recent sessions, never against a fixed number.

Then the count — advancers against decliners. It is the cheapest truth on the board. An index down three points with three decliners for every advancer is not the same day as an index down three points with the count evenly split, however identical the headlines look the following morning.

And the candles. A yang line, drawn red, closes above its open; a yin line, drawn green, closes below. The wicks above and below record where the session pushed and was pushed back — the distance between the price a market achieved and the price it was willing to hold. Whether a body is drawn hollow or filled is the software vendor's choice, not the market's; two terminals can look different and say the same thing.

Limit-up and limit-down read as stillness. The price stops moving and the queue sits on one side of it: buyers stacked behind a ceiling, sellers stacked behind a floor. The mechanism was described in Pre 02. On a screen, the signal is not the number; it is the fact that the number has stopped changing.

FOUR Two Green Days Old accounts
24 Aug 2015:
−8.49 percent.
3 Feb 2020:
−7.72 percent.

Old account one. On 24 August 2015 the Shanghai Composite closed at 3,209.91, down 8.49 percent — a session in which forced deleveraging and thinning liquidity fed each other, and in which the screen was as green as it ever gets. One difference worth recording: that day's damage came from inside the market's own plumbing, from borrowed money being recalled; a screen that green means something quite different when the cause is external.

Old account two. 3 February 2020, the session this chapter opened with: 2,746.61, down 7.72 percent, 3,016 names sealed at the floor. One difference worth recording: that fall compressed a holiday's worth of news into a single opening print, at a moment when the shock was still being measured; it was an exogenous event priced in one gulp, not a structure coming apart.

What the two days share is the thing worth keeping. On both, the greenest screen of the year was also the worst session of it. And the mirror image holds: on 30 September 2024, the Shanghai Composite closed at 3,336.50, up 8.06 percent, with more than seven hundred names sealed at the ceiling — the reddest screen in years, and the best day in it. Colour here is not a warning or a promise. It is a dialect, and it only tells you which way the crowd walked.
