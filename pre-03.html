ONE The Morning You Cannot Leave Opening
On 28 Sept 2026,
the opening print
was the high
of the entire day.

Monday, 28 September 2026, was the first session after the Mid-Autumn holiday, and the Shanghai Composite opened as if it had brought good news back from the long weekend: 3,878.41. That number turned out to be the high of the entire day. By the close the index sat at 3,823.62, down 1.67 percent, and every sector tied to artificial-intelligence hardware had given back several days of gains in a single afternoon.

Consider the person who bought during that optimistic first hour. In most markets, the trade has gone badly and the trade can be undone; a click, a fee, done. Here the click does nothing. The shares bought that morning could not be sold until the next trading day. What was meant to be a position of minutes became a position of nights, and the night had a bill attached.

This is the rule locals call T+1 — shares purchased today may not be sold until tomorrow, next trading day. Cash from a sale can be reused the same afternoon, but the stock itself is locked in until the next opening bell. It sounds like an administrative footnote. It is closer to the load-bearing wall of the entire market's temperament, and this chapter is about why.

TWO Where the Rule Came From 1992–1995
Under T+0 in
1992–93, the index
doubled in about
seven weeks.

Like the price wall described in the last chapter, T+1 was not the founding design. It was a patch, bolted on after something went wrong. In the young market of the early 1990s, Shanghai allowed same-day round trips — the regime the world calls T+0 — from May 1992, and Shenzhen followed in November 1993. For a while a stock could be bought and sold the same morning, repeatedly, by the same hands.

What followed is the part every veteran of this market can recite. Between late December 1992 and mid-February 1993, the Shanghai index ran from around 760 to above 1,558 — a doubling in roughly seven weeks, driven by a market in which hot money could turn over without pause. The regulators read that episode the way a physician reads a fever chart. On 1 January 1995, both exchanges reverted to T+1, and the rule has held for more than thirty years.

It has been challenged often. The 1998 Securities Law wrote the prohibition into statute; the 2005 revision deleted the clause, which touched off a decade of speculation about a return to T+0; in 2013 the Shanghai exchange itself published a call to study reintroducing it, and in 2020 it proposed a carefully limited version for the new STAR board. None of it happened. The STAR Market arrived in 2019 with its daily limits widened to 20 percent — and kept T+1. Even the market's youngest, most professional board still keeps the old lock.

Old Account · No. 1 — The First Days of the Lock

The first session under T+1, 3 January 1995, was mild on its face: the Shanghai Composite fell 1.23 percent, Shenzhen's component index 0.11 percent. What followed was quieter and worse. Turnover contracted sharply as the same-day crowd left, and the index drifted lower for five weeks, touching 524 in early February 1995 — from a 1994 close of 647, a slide of roughly 19 percent.

One difference worth recording: the 1995 market was tiny, thinly capitalized, and had just lived through a same-day-trading boom, so its dependency on the T+0 crowd was far greater than today's institutionalized market's would be. The lesson is about the mechanism, not the magnitude.

The record shows where the road has been, not where it goes next.
THREE The Long Night Mechanism
In other markets
you can change
your mind. Here,
your mind changes
tomorrow.

To a reader from a market without the rule, the deepest consequence is not liquidity. It is that a morning purchase in Shanghai is a wager that includes the night. American markets tolerate overnight risk too — gaps between close and open are universal — but the American trader who regrets a position by lunchtime can end it by lunchtime. The Shanghai trader cannot. Every trade here is, structurally, an overnight trade, even one executed at 9:31 in the morning.

This is why the overnight gap, the space between one close and the next open, carries such weight in the local vocabulary. And it is why the market's memory keeps returning to certain mornings.

Old Account · No. 2 — The Gate That Opened Too Wide

On 8 October 2024, the first session after that year's Golden Week, the Shanghai Composite gapped up by more than ten percent at the open — after the pre-holiday session had already closed up 8.06 percent on record turnover of ¥2.59 trillion. The euphoria faded within the day and the index finished 4.59 percent higher. The next session, 9 October, the index fell 6.62 percent. Buyers who had chased the opening print of the eighth were locked in by T+1 through the close of the ninth; the earliest they could act on what they were watching was the tenth.

One difference worth recording: that week was the fastest policy-driven re-rating in a decade, with turnover at unprecedented scale — gaps of that width are the exception that justifies the rule, not the rule's daily work. Most sessions open within a fraction of a percent of the prior close.

The record shows where the road has been, not where it goes next.

Once you see it, T+1 explains a great deal of the market's observed behavior. It is one reason sentiment that cannot be spent today gets banked until tomorrow, so strength and weakness both run a day longer than a free-exit market would allow. It is one reason the tape goes quiet before long holidays: a position taken before a multi-day closure carries not one night but several, and this chronicle has watched turnover fall to a fraction of its recent norm in the final sessions before National Day week, year after year.

It also shapes the language. When local commentators describe a failed rally, they say the buyers got trapped on the mountain — a phrase that only makes sense in a market where getting off the mountain is scheduled, not chosen.

Every market has a speed limit somewhere. Here, the limit is not on the price. It is on the change of mind.

The poet Cavafy wrote that you will not find a new country, because you carry the old one with you. This market has made that sentence operational. Tomorrow is not simply another day here; it is another country, with its own opening price, and you cannot cross the border before it opens.
