ONE The Dry Riverbed Opening

Four months,

On the afternoon of 18 September 2024, the first session back from the Mid-Autumn break, the Shanghai Composite broke 2,700. The day's low print was 2,689.70 — the deepest point of a slide that had been running since May. By the close the index had been hauled back to 2,717.28, up 0.49 percent, lifted in two late waves by buying in the broad index funds: the kind of rescue that gets a market through an afternoon and promises nothing beyond it.

The tape had gone quiet in a way traders noticed before they could explain. Combined Shanghai-Shenzhen turnover was ¥479 billion, the third-thinnest day of the year. More than 3,600 stocks fell; around 1,500 rose. The index had closed at 3,174.27 on 20 May — fifteen percent higher, four months earlier — and it had been roughly three years since it last saw 3,500.

That night, in the small hours of Beijing time, the Federal Reserve cut rates by half a point, its first cut of the cycle, and the People's Bank of China had room it had been lacking. What the PBoC would do with the room was, on that date, unknown. Analysts told reporters that valuations had reached historical lows — true, and useless as a timetable, which is the usual trouble with true things. The market was waiting the way a riverbed waits: dry, and shaped for water.

A difference worth recording
Dryness in September 2024 was despair at the end of a four-month slide, with the policy toolbox still shut. A thin tape can also occur at mid-levels, for calendar reasons alone; the record of one year does not adjudicate another.

Early on a Tuesday morning six days later, the governor of the central bank, the banking regulator and the securities regulator sat down at one table before the press — the three of them together being itself unusual — and opened the toolbox. The list: a half-point cut in banks' reserve requirement, releasing about ¥1 trillion; the policy rate lowered from 1.7 to 1.5 percent; existing mortgage rates trimmed by around half a point, worth some ¥150 billion a year to households; minimum down payments unified at 15 percent; and two new facilities built for the stock market — a ¥500 billion swap line letting securities, fund and insurance firms finance share purchases against their assets, and a ¥300 billion re-lending window for buybacks. The central bank, in other words, had begun installing itself in the market's plumbing.

The answer took one session. The index rose 4.15 percent to 2,863.13, its largest day in four years. Turnover jumped by ¥422 billion in a day, to ¥974 billion. Ninety-seven stocks in every hundred rose.

Then came the second act, which no calendar had scheduled. On 26 September the Politburo met on the economy — a gathering that normally waits for April, July and December — and said in plain language that the capital market was to be revived and long-term money guided in. The index rose 3.61 percent and closed above 3,000 for the first time in 68 trading days. On the 27th the reserve cut took effect, and the Shanghai exchange's matching engine, asked to process the feelings of the entire country at once, stalled: from around 10:10 to 11:13 the index drew a flat line at 3,030, orders went unconfirmed, and the exchange apologised twice before the close. Even so the day ended 2.89 percent higher at 3,087.53, with 137 stocks pinned limit-up — sealed at the ten-percent ceiling described in Pre 02 — and the ChiNext, Shenzhen's growth board, up ten percent, then a record.

The month's last session was the loud one. Up 8.06 percent, the largest daily gain since October 2008, the index closed at 3,336.50. Turnover reached ¥2.59 trillion, breaking a record that had stood since May 2015; ¥1 trillion of it arrived inside the first 35 minutes. The eight sessions since 18 September's close had added 23 percent. The three-year fall from 3,500 to 2,700 had been retraced in six sessions — one session for every six months of decline.

A difference worth recording
Five sessions of that width came from a dated, itemised package landing after a four-month washout. The figures describe the week the toolbox opened. They are not a description of thinness, or of policy, in general.

Golden Week — the National Day closure — intervened: a week in which Hong Kong, still trading, rallied hard while the mainland sat closed and every dinner table discussed the market. When Shanghai reopened on 8 October, the index itself gapped 10.13 percent higher, opening at 3,674, at what amounted to the market-wide ceiling: thousands of stocks opened pinned at their own ten-percent limits, several brokers' apps gave up under the load, and ¥1 trillion changed hands in the first twenty minutes. On the same morning, Hong Kong fell nine percent. Shanghai sobered slowly: the index faded to a gain of barely one percent late in the morning, recovered, and closed up 4.59 percent at 3,489.78 on ¥3.45 trillion — the loudest day in the market's history. Seven hundred and ninety-one stocks finished limit-up.

The morning peak was never revisited. The next session the index fell 6.62 percent, back below 3,300, and the ChiNext fell 10.59 percent — the worst day in its history. More than 5,000 stocks declined, over 800 of them pinned limit-down. Turnover was still ¥2.94 trillion, the second-loudest day ever recorded: the crowd that had rushed in stayed long enough to learn where the exits were. It was the first trading day for many accounts opened during the holiday, and several brokers marked the occasion with public letters urging their new customers, in effect, not to borrow.

What followed was two weeks of digestion, churn rather than trend. On 18 October the index stood at 3,261.56, up 2.91 percent that day on ¥2.1 trillion of trade. Set against the landmarks of the preceding fortnight: about eleven percent below the morning peak of 8 October; about two percent below the September close; still more than a fifth above the September low. The giveback was smaller than the advance, and the floor held far above where the story had started — but the cost of the trip included the worst single day the growth board had ever printed, one session after its best.

The record shows where the road has been, not where it goes next.
A difference worth recording
Between the peak and the landing lay eight sessions containing one −6.62 percent day and the growth board's historical worst. A re-run of the level would not be a re-run of the path — and the path is what a record actually contains.

Chapter One.

This column's daily chapters begin on 8 October 2026 — two years to the day after the morning Shanghai opened ten percent higher. The date is coincidence; the choice of story is not. Nine days in the autumn of 2024 contain most of what is worth knowing about this market's temperament: how quickly it reprices when the toolbox opens, what the ten-percent wall and the T+1 lock — Pre 02 and Pre 03's subjects — do to that speed, and how loud the room can be on the day it is most wrong as well as the day it is most right. The chapters ahead will write the tape one day at a time, on the record. Markets keep short memories and excellent records; a writer who cannot predict them can at least keep the records company.
